Mathematical Design and Lathe Machining of Class-M Rocket Nozzles

Published: May 15, 2026 · By: Nitesh Neupane · 5 min read

The nozzle of a solid rocket motor is the critical boundary component where chemical combustion energy is converted into kinetic thrust. Through a convergent-divergent (CD or de Laval) nozzle profile, subsonic exhaust gases generated in the motor casing are compressed, choked at the throat to sonic velocity ($M=1$), and expanded supersonically into the atmosphere. For amateur rocketry projects, such as the Class-M propellant motor engineered by Kathmandu University's **Team Udaan**, designing and fabricating a nozzle demands high-precision gas dynamics modeling, thermal material considerations, and strict machining tolerances. This article details the mathematical design flow and manual lathe-turning processes used to manufacture a high-performance rocket nozzle.

1. Gas Dynamics & Nozzle Sizing

The primary task of nozzle sizing is to determine the throat area ($A_t$) and exit area ($A_e$) for a target chamber pressure ($P_c$). The throat area dictates the mass flow rate ($\dot{m}$) of the motor and, consequently, the casing internal pressure. If the throat is too wide, chamber pressure remains low, resulting in a poor propellant burn rate and low specific impulse ($I_{sp}$). If the throat is too narrow, chamber pressure will exceed the design yield strength of the motor casing, causing a catastrophic failure (a "CATO").

Using the steady-state mass balance, the throat area is calculated as:

A_t = (A_b * r * rho_p) / (P_c * C_star)

Where $A_b$ is the burning surface area of the propellant, $r$ is the linear propellant burn rate, $\rho_p$ is the propellant density, and $C^*$ is the characteristic velocity of the gas. For our Class-M solid propellant (a Potassium Nitrate and Sorbitol mixture, commonly known as KNSB candy propellant), we targeted a maximum chamber pressure of $4.5\text{ MPa}$ ($650\text{ psi}$).

The expansion ratio ($\epsilon = A_e / A_t$) defines how much the gas expands. Under ideal gas expansion equations, the exit pressure of the gas ($P_e$) should equal the local atmospheric pressure ($P_a$) at the target burn altitude to prevent over-expansion (loss of exit velocity) or under-expansion (exhaust energy wasted as radial pressure).

The geometry of the nozzle features two cones:

  • Converging Section: Formed as a cone with a half-angle of 30 to 45 degrees. A steeper angle keeps the nozzle short but can lead to flow turbulence, while a shallow angle increases nozzle mass.
  • Diverging Section: Machined with a half-angle of 12 to 15 degrees. This angle is a critical compromise: exceeding 15 degrees risks gas boundary-layer separation from the nozzle walls (creating shocks and loss of thrust), while a smaller angle increases the length and mass of the nozzle.

2. Simulation with OpenMotor and Richard Nakka Models

We verified our manual calculations using two key tools: **Richard Nakka's Solid Rocket Motor design spreadsheet** and **OpenMotor** (an open-source interior ballistics simulator). By inputting our motor dimensions, casing ID, and propellant grain geometry (four BATES grains), OpenMotor simulated the transient burn. Since solid propellant grains burn radially outward, the burning area ($A_b$) changes over time, altering the chamber pressure and nozzle mass flow. The simulation predicted a peak thrust of $1,400\text{ N}$ with a total burn duration of 2.1 seconds, yielding a total impulse corresponding to a mid-range Class-M rocket motor.

3. Material Selection & Lathe Machining

Exhaust temperatures for KNSB propellant reach approximately $1,600^\circ\text{C}$ ($2,900^\circ\text{F}$), combined with high-velocity abrasive particulate flow (primarily liquid potassium carbonate droplets). For a reusable nozzle, the material must withstand these conditions without throat erosion, which would enlarge $A_t$ and drop chamber pressure during the burn.

We selected **Mild Steel (AISI 1018)** for the nozzle body because of its high melting point, thermal conductivity, and machinability on Kathmandu University's manual lathe. To completely eliminate throat erosion, we designed the nozzle with a press-fit **graphite throat insert**, which acts as a sacrificial thermal shield. The machining workflow on the lathe proceeded as follows:

  1. Outer Diameter Turning: A solid round bar of steel was mounted in a 3-jaw chuck, faced, and turned to match the internal diameter of the motor casing, leaving groove recesses for high-temperature rubber O-rings.
  2. Boring the Throat & Insert Seat: A pilot hole was drilled through the center. We then bored a counterbore seat to hold the cylindrical graphite insert. The graphite insert itself was turned to fit this seat with an interference fit of $0.02\text{ mm}$.
  3. Convergent Angle Machining: The lathe compound slide was rotated to $30^\circ$ relative to the spindle axis. Using manual feed, we cut the convergent entry cone leading to the graphite throat.
  4. Divergent Cone Machining: The compound slide was reset to $12^\circ$. Using a boring bar with a sharp carbide insert, we machined the diverging cone, widening the nozzle exit to the calculated exit diameter.

4. Verification via Static Testing

We verified the completed nozzle design through a series of **static fire tests**. The motor assembly was anchored in a horizontal steel testing rig. Thrust was measured using a pre-calibrated S-beam load cell connected to an Arduino-based data acquisition system recording at $80\text{ Hz}$. The static test results showed a smooth thrust curve peaking at $1,345\text{ N}$, with a chamber pressure envelope matching our OpenMotor projections within 5.2%. Post-test inspection of the nozzle confirmed zero visible erosion at the graphite throat throat and intact O-ring seals, qualifying the nozzle design for integration into Team Udaan's flight vehicles.
